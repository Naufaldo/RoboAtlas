# 2D Geometry & Planar Coordinate Transforms in $SE(2)$

> "How does a robot know where an obstacle is in the world when its sensor only measures distance relative to itself?"

Every autonomous robot consists of multiple physical parts: a chassis body, wheels, sensors (like LiDAR or cameras), and end-effectors. Each part defines its own local coordinate system.

---

## 1. The Multi-Frame Dilemma in Robotics

Consider a mobile robot $\{R\}$ equipped with a laser scanner $\{S\}$ operating in a room with a global world frame $\{W\}$:

1. The LiDAR sensor detects a wall landmark at local coordinate $\mathbf{p}^S = [1.2, 0.5]^T \text{ m}$.
2. The LiDAR is mounted on the robot chassis at an offset $\mathbf{t}_{S}^R = [0.3, 0.0]^T \text{ m}$.
3. The robot itself is located in the room at position $\mathbf{t}_R^W = [4.0, 2.5]^T \text{ m}$ with heading orientation $\theta = 45^\circ$.

To place the obstacle on the global map, the robot must convert $\mathbf{p}^S \to \mathbf{p}^R \to \mathbf{p}^W$.

---

## 2. 2D Rotation Matrices in $SO(2)$

When a robot rotates by heading angle $\theta$, every point $\mathbf{p} = [x, y]^T$ rotates around the origin according to trigonometric projection:

$$
R(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}
$$

Key properties of rotation matrices:
- **Orthogonality**: $R^{-1}(\theta) = R^T(\theta) = R(-\theta)$
- **Unit Determinant**: $\det(R) = +1$ (preserves distances and angles without scaling or mirroring).
- **Special Orthogonal Group**: $R \in SO(2)$.

---

## 3. Homogeneous Transformations in $SE(2)$

In standard Euclidean space, rotation is multiplicative ($\mathbf{p}' = R\mathbf{p}$) while translation is additive ($\mathbf{p}^W = \mathbf{p}' + \mathbf{t}$). Combining them requires an affine operation:

$$
\mathbf{p}^W = R \mathbf{p}^R + \mathbf{t}
$$

To compose multiple transformations using pure matrix multiplication, we augment coordinates into **homogeneous coordinates** by appending a trailing $1$:

$$
\mathbf{p} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

The combined transformation matrix $T \in SE(2)$ (Special Euclidean Group) is structured as:

$$
T_R^W = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & t_x \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & t_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Multiplying $T_R^W$ by a local point $\mathbf{p}^R$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} x^W \\ y^W \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & t_x \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & t_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x^R \\ y^R \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x^R\cos\theta - y^R\sin\theta + t_x \\ x^R\sin\theta + y^R\cos\theta + t_y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

## 5. Composition Chains & Inverse Transforms

### Transformation Chaining
Transformations compose naturally through matrix multiplication from right to left:

$$
T_S^W = T_R^W T_S^R
$$

### Analytical Matrix Inverse
To transform world coordinates back into robot coordinates $(\mathbf{p}^R = (T_R^W)^{-1}\mathbf{p}^W)$, we compute the closed-form inverse without expensive matrix inversion:

$$
(T_R^W)^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} R^T & -R^T \mathbf{t} \\ \mathbf{0}^T & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta & -(t_x\cos\theta + t_y\sin\theta) \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta & -(-t_x\sin\theta + t_y\cos\theta) \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$
