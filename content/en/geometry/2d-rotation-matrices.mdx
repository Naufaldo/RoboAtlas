# 2D Spatial Rotation & Special Orthogonal Group SO(2) (M03)

> "When a robot rotates its chassis or an articulated arm bends at a joint, how do we transform coordinates without distorting physical geometry?"

Rotations in robotics must preserve lengths and angles between vectors (rigid-body motion). In 2D planar space, rotations belong to the Lie group known as the **Special Orthogonal Group $\text{SO}(2)$**.

---

## 1. Step-by-Step Trigonometric Derivation

Consider an initial 2D point $P = [x, y]^T$ located at distance $r$ and angle $\alpha$ from origin:

$$
x = r \cos\alpha, \qquad y = r \sin\alpha
$$

Rotating point $P$ counter-clockwise by angle $\theta$ to new point $P' = [x', y']^T$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x' &= r \cos(\alpha + \theta) \\
y' &= r \sin(\alpha + \theta)
\end{aligned}
$$

Applying the trigonometric angle-sum identities:
- $\cos(\alpha + \theta) = \cos\alpha \cos\theta - \sin\alpha \sin\theta$
- $\sin(\alpha + \theta) = \sin\alpha \cos\theta + \cos\alpha \sin\theta$

Substituting $x = r\cos\alpha$ and $y = r\sin\alpha$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x' &= x \cos\theta - y \sin\theta \\
y' &= x \sin\theta + y \cos\theta
\end{aligned}
$$

Writing this system in matrix-vector form gives the **2D Rotation Matrix $R(\theta) \in \text{SO}(2)$**:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}
$$

---

## 2. Fundamental Properties of SO(2) Matrices

Any valid rotation matrix $R \in \text{SO}(2)$ satisfies three core mathematical properties:

1. **Orthogonality Property (Trivial Inverse)**:
   $$
   R^T R = I \implies R^{-1} = R^T = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} = R(-\theta)
   $$
   *(Inverting a rotation matrix is simply taking its matrix transpose!)*

2. **Unit Determinant (No Reflections)**:
   $$
   \det(R) = \cos^2\theta - (-\sin^2\theta) = \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = +1
   $$

3. **Norm Preservation (Rigid Body Isometry)**:
   $$
   \|R \mathbf{v}\| = \|\mathbf{v}\| \quad \forall \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^2
   $$
